# Understand and apply the six criteria

The foundational definitions come from Walter Murch's Rule of Six. The questions, diagnostics, and procedures below are an **AI-oriented application framework**, synthesized from editing practice and cognition research; they are not an original workflow prescribed by Murch. Consult [sources and limitations](sources.md) when checking attribution.

Contents: [Emotion](#emotion), [Story](#story), [Rhythm](#rhythm), [Eye-trace](#eye-trace), [2D screen plane](#2d-screen-plane), [3D action space](#3d-action-space), [Tradeoffs](#tradeoffs-between-criteria).

## Emotion

**Meaning.** Emotion concerns the experience intended for the audience at a particular moment: intimacy, suspicion, tension, relief, curiosity, distance, or a deliberate mixture. It differs from a character's displayed emotion. A smiling character can make viewers uneasy when they already know about a threat. Murch places emotion first in his editorial priorities. His accompanying percentages emphasize that priority rather than establish a universal psychological response. ([Murch excerpt at UC Berkeley](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-viznarr-s12/the-rule-of-six-walter-murch/))

**What the AI can observe.** Record specific behavior and sound: a speaker stops mid-sentence, changes gaze direction, grips something, or delays answering; the framing moves closer or keeps its distance; a sound begins or stops. Interpret afterward and conditionally. “The character lowers their face” is checkable; “the character is definitely remorseful” requires further support. Do not infer a person's actual emotional state from one facial expression.

**Editorial questions.** Whose perspective is the audience aligned with? Should viewers know as much as a character or know something first? Does the cut reveal a change too early, at the useful moment, or too late? Does an additional hold develop the moment or merely prolong an already clear state? Would a listener's reaction reveal the consequence of the words? Is music supporting a nuance or imposing meaning unsupported by the footage?

**Actions to try.** Select a take for its performance development rather than an attractive isolated frame; hold while a reaction forms; move to the listener when receiving information is the focus; reduce competing music or graphics; change the order of revelation when the story permits. Separate picture and sound cuts to retain speech while showing a reaction. Evaluate the choice with surrounding context: the same reaction can acquire different meanings beside different words.

**Warning signs.** Cutting before a change can be read; removing every silence; using constant close-ups without emotional development; choosing music first and forcing performance onto its beats; inserting a compelling reaction from the wrong factual context. A stronger emotion is not automatically the appropriate emotion.

**Fictional example.** Someone receiving an apology pauses, then relaxes their hand. If the intended change is growing acceptance, holding until that movement may be more useful than cutting wide as the apology ends. If acceptance should remain uncertain, staying with the speaker or leaving earlier may serve the scene. Without footage, the more effective timing cannot be finalized.

**Limits.** The AI predicts an experience from intent and cues. Appropriate audience data is needed to establish how a tested group actually responds. Do not attach certainty or an unmeasured engagement increase to a proposed edit.

## Story

**Meaning.** Story organizes information and meaningful relationships: who wants what, which obstacle they face, what changes, why, with what consequences, and who knows what. A tutorial may progress from problem to action to result; an interview may develop an argument; a montage may advance an idea or motif. A pause can advance the story when it changes how viewers understand a person or situation.

**What the AI can observe.** List information actually present in the source, open questions, setups, payoffs, developments, point of view, and repetition. Separate recorded events from causal relationships created by juxtaposition. When using a shot as evidence, verify that it belongs to the event being discussed.

**Editorial questions.** What do viewers know before this beat? What will they learn or reinterpret afterward? Is this shot necessary to understand the next action? Is a removed detail a prerequisite for the result? Is a time compression legible, or does it falsely suggest uninterrupted action? Is an answer revealed before the audience forms the question? Does joining two statements alter the speaker's meaning?

**Actions to try.** Map changes in understanding; preserve essential setup; remove redundant information; reorganize an explanation within truthful limits; select B-roll that demonstrates the spoken content or adds a clear layer of meaning. Use sound bridges when they preserve the argument. For multiple strands, establish whether a cut signals a new strand, simultaneous action, or a flashback clearly enough.

**Warning signs.** Attractive scenes without a function; a crucial name or prop without sufficient introduction; an essential step removed; a reaction reassigned to another question; novelty without progression. Removing a shot may solve the problem more effectively than decorating its transition.

**Fictional example.** A latch tutorial shows the starting state and the locked result but omits the essential turning action. Viewers may be unable to repeat it even if the edit is short and visually smooth. Retain actual turning coverage or identify a pickup needed if it does not exist; do not claim that additional footage has already been supplied.

**Limits.** Fiction can intentionally mislead and later resolve uncertainty. Documentary, news, interviews, and instruction have their own factual requirements; artistic priorities do not authorize changing statements or inventing causality. Follow the project's purpose and actual source material.

## Rhythm

**Meaning.** Rhythm organizes change and time at three scales: within a shot, between shots, and across the scene or video. Internal rhythm comes from performance, speech, breath, movement, camera action, and waiting. Inter-shot rhythm comes from duration, order, repetition, and contrast. Larger rhythm develops through intensification, release, and phase changes. A dramatic beat changes a goal, tactic, or piece of information; a musical beat is a unit of music. They can coincide without being identical. ([Karen Pearlman's author article](https://www.karenpearlman.net/karenpearlmannet/keeping-emotions-moving.html))

**Craft basis.** Murch describes drawing rhythm from performance and the scene, and testing whether its visual/dramatic structure stands before adding temp music. This is a craft method rather than a requirement that music videos or performance pieces must always be assembled picture-first. ([CineMontage interview](https://cinemontage.org/visual-music-walter-murch/))

**What the AI can observe.** Mark changes in meaning, the end of emphasis, hesitation, action starts/turns/completions, reactions, and sound-layer changes. Identify what each hold accomplishes. A static shot can contain a significant development; a highly active shot can repeat information.

**Editorial questions.** What must viewers wait for before the cut? Does the edit clip a consonant, breath, or meaning? Are too many reading demands simultaneous? Is regularity intentional or a by-product of equal shot lengths? Without music, are the dialogue and action coherent? When music leads the brief, have phrasing and climaxes been considered beyond individual beats?

**Actions to try.** Compare cuts before, at, and after a change; hold one take across an exchange; restore breathing room at a shift in meaning; trim material that no longer develops; use an understandable ellipsis; organize a montage into purposeful groups. Consider J/L-cuts when sound can lead meaning. A J-cut brings incoming sound before its picture; an L-cut continues outgoing sound over the next picture. Neither specifies a default overlap duration. ([Adobe's J/L-cut documentation](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/edit-projects/trim-clips/perform-j-cuts-and-l-cuts.html))

**Warning signs.** Cutting at every speaker change or every musical beat; eliminating all pauses; repeating an action because phases do not match; using loud music to conceal unnatural dialogue timing. Cut counts and average shot length describe only part of the structure and cannot select the correct cut by themselves.

**Fictional example.** Someone hears surprising information, pauses, and asks for confirmation. A musical-beat cut to another scene before the question may lose the dramatic consequence. Try retaining the question and adjusting the musical transition, or omit it if another genuine reaction serves that function. Choose after reviewing the complete passage with sound.

**Research limits.** Magliano and Zacks studied event segmentation in a particular film, not automatic frame selection. Cutting found correlations between music, shot-duration patterns, and perceived sequence boundaries, not social-platform retention. These findings do not establish a cut-every-N-seconds rule. ([Magliano & Zacks, 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208769/), [Cutting, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6675763/))

## Eye-trace

**Meaning.** Eye-trace concerns the audience's area of interest and attention shift across a cut. Eyeline concerns a character's gaze direction and is one possible attention cue. A character may look toward a door while the audience watches a hand conceal a key. Smith's attentional theory explains continuity through coordination of expectations and attention cues; it does not require every viewer to maintain the same spatial map or gaze pattern. ([Smith, 2012, author repository](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21187/))

**What the AI can observe.** First identify the narrative question viewers are following. Then identify the necessary object at the end of A and start of B; cues such as speech, gaze, movement, contrast, close framing, or text; competing regions; and cropped-out information. A broad region is often more useful than falsely precise coordinates.

**Editorial questions.** Whom or what must viewers find after the cut? Do cues help recognition? Does a new caption compete with a small action? Has the crop removed the target of a character's look? Should attention continue or deliberately reset? Does a change in focus have a purpose and adequate reception time in actual playback?

**Actions to try.** Choose an angle that makes the next object identifiable; adjust the cut around a gesture; reduce nonessential graphics; split text into meaningful chunks; reframe to retain hands or props; hold for inspection; use movement or sound as a cue. Do not require every focus to remain at the same location or at screen center.

**Warning signs.** Viewers must search for crucial information after it has passed; animated text attracts more attention than the central evidence; centering a person removes the product; the AI labels predicted saliency as measured gaze. Subtitle eye-tracking research shows that text can alter attention allocation under the studied conditions, without establishing a universal layout rule. ([Joy & Padakannaya, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10723748/))

**Fictional example.** Shot A shows a hand placing a button in the lower right. Shot B moves close to the button, but a large caption appears over it. Try moving text to available negative space, dividing the caption, or delaying secondary graphics. Preserve necessary captions while finding a way to communicate both speech and action.

**Limits.** Label the focus as a hypothesis without actual eye tracking. For coordinates, record aspect/crop, source frame, estimation type, and coordinate system. Avoid universal thresholds in pixels, milliseconds, or attention displacement. Unseen video supports checks to perform rather than confirmed visual findings.

## 2D screen plane

**Meaning.** This criterion concerns the projection of a three-dimensional world onto a flat image: left/right, eyelines, action direction, and axis relationships between camera angles. Murch directly explains it through the 180-degree rule. Keeping cameras on one side of an axis generally preserves screen orientation; crossing can make characters appear to switch sides. ([Art of the Cut interview](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-murch-books/), [Columbia, 180-Degree Rule](https://filmglossary.ccnmtl.columbia.edu/term/180-degree-rule/))

**What the AI can observe.** Record each subject's screen side, eyeline and movement directions, camera-axis relationship when identifiable, and relative placement across available angles. Do not invent a precise camera diagram from insufficient images. A scene with several people or moving subjects may change its active axis; inspect the context rather than assigning one fixed line to the whole video.

**Editorial questions.** Do the characters appear to look toward each other as intended? Does a runner seem to reverse course accidentally? Does the new angle establish a new viewpoint or simply confuse direction? What emotional or informational benefit motivates a perspective change? Before declaring a fault, does surrounding footage show movement that explains it?

**Actions to try.** Choose a same-side angle when continuity matters; use suitable re-establishing coverage; show a camera or subject crossing when footage exists; test a near-axis angle to introduce another orientation. These are candidates to evaluate, not guaranteed fixes. A neutral shot does not automatically resolve every reversal. Mirroring can alter text, handedness, props, and directional meaning, so it is not the default remedy.

**Warning signs.** Dialogue partners suddenly face the same way without purpose; running direction changes because the camera side changes; reframing sends a look toward the wrong off-screen target; individually attractive angles become confusing in sequence. An easy eye-trace transition does not prove eyeline or axis continuity.

**Fictional example.** A is screen-left looking right; B is screen-right looking left. A crossing angle makes B look right. For straightforward dialogue, try a same-side angle or coverage introducing the new side. For a power shift intended to unsettle viewers, the reversal may be worth testing with a specific purpose and a plan for reorientation.

**Limits.** Composition, color, and typography support modern video, but they are applications beyond the original definition; 2D planarity should not be reduced to all graphic design. The 180-degree rule is a convention that can be deliberately broken, not a physical law.

## 3D action space

**Meaning.** This criterion concerns coherent relationships in the scene's world: who is where relative to whom, doors and obstacles, sightlines, and paths of movement. In application, track action state and phase where they matter for intelligible spatial/temporal continuity. It does not require a 3D model or stereoscopic output.

**What the AI can observe.** Build a sufficient relationship map: A faces B, a door is behind B, A holds the cup, a runner heads toward stairs. Track what affects meaning: position, route, obstacles, held objects, interaction direction, and action phase. Avoid claiming real-world distances, focal lengths, or compass bearings from insufficient frames.

**Editorial questions.** Can viewers infer the actor and destination? After the cut, is the action continuing, time being omitted, or a new event beginning? Does an object teleport or a movement repeat accidentally? If positions change, is there a cue? Does the discrepancy affect understanding or remain a minor detail?

**Actions to try.** Retain or replace establishment when relationships matter; re-establish after movement; match action phase and direction; preserve information needed for the next step; make ellipses readable; bridge sound only when it connects the intended event. Establishing shots, eyeline matches, shot/reverse-shot, and match-on-action work together. ([Filmmakers Academy, Continuity Editing](https://www.filmmakersacademy.com/glossary/continuity-editing/), [CCEA teacher guidance](https://ccea.org.uk/downloads/docs/Support/General/2019/Moving%20Image%20Arts%20-%20Teacher%20Guidance.pdf))

**Warning signs.** A crucial object changes hands without a transfer; someone appears beyond an obstacle while the scene implies continuous action; a missing procedure makes the result unintelligible; an audio bridge falsely implies simultaneous locations. A small prop discrepancy can become a major story issue when it is a clue.

**Fictional example.** Two characters retain readable screen positions, but a key moves from A's hand to B's without a transfer. 2D orientation and eye-trace may remain clear while action continuity fails. Choose coverage of the transfer or make a time omission clear if the story permits.

**Limits.** Montages, memories, and graphic sequences need not imply one continuous space. Use `not_applicable` with a reason when conceptual linkage is the main structure. Two isolated frames do not prove an error if earlier action explains the change.

## Tradeoffs between criteria

Separate **task requirements** from **artistic priorities**. When competing options preserve meaning, evidence, and delivery requirements, use Murch's hierarchy to guide judgment. An option that misrepresents essential information cannot be treated as a harmless continuity sacrifice.

| Conflict | A checkable approach |
|---|---|
| Stronger performance with a small prop discrepancy | Determine whether the mismatch confuses the story; test retaining it when the specific performance gain matters and action stays clear. |
| A musical-beat cut loses a reaction | Compare preserving the reaction while adjusting music; decide from scene intent rather than the beat alone. |
| A smooth cut removes an explanatory step | Restore the necessary information so the video fulfills its purpose. |
| An axis reversal shocks but causes prolonged confusion | Identify its benefit, intended disorientation, and available reorientation coverage; remove it if the confusion is unintended. |
| An attractive shot has captions covering the action | Adjust layout, crop, or graphic timing; inspect the final output with sound and text. |

Explain a consequential choice as **evidence → intended audience change → decision → cost → verification**. Avoid lengthy internal deliberation or mandatory six-part scoring for an ordinary small change.
